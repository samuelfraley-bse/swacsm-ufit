**Where Should a Strength Program Spend a Limited Wearable Data-Collection Budget?**

**PURPOSE:** Wrist-worn accelerometers can automatically log resistance training, but a program adopting one must first record labeled examples, and high-turnover programs repeatedly onboard new athletes. We examined how a limited recording budget should be allocated, and whether low-cost shortcuts can substitute for recording athletes.

**METHODS:** Secondary analysis of a public dataset in which 35 adults performed 15 exercises (2,355 sets) wearing a single wrist-worn 3-axis accelerometer. Twenty-three participants recorded on at least 2 days were included. In each of 30 random draws, 6 participants were held out as never-recorded "new athletes", and simulated programs recorded 2 to 17 other participants for 1 or 2 sets per exercise on their first day. Recognition was tested on later days only, using a random forest, a convolutional neural network (CNN) and a long short-term memory network. Shortcuts tested were: recording a new athlete on only 2 exercises, extra sets of the most-confused lifts, label-free calibration from one unlabeled session, and self-training on unlabeled teammate data. Signals were also split into posture (gravity) and movement components. The outcome was set-level macro-F1 (0-1). Participants were the unit of analysis (paired Wilcoxon tests; 95% bootstrap confidence intervals).

**RESULTS:** For new athletes, random-forest macro-F1 rose from 0.64 (95% CI 0.60-0.67) with 2 recorded athletes to 0.84 (0.80-0.87) with 17; about 12 athletes captured 90% of this gain. The random forest performed best with 2 recorded athletes and the CNN with 17 (0.90 vs 0.86, p = 0.001). At an equal number of recorded sets, recording twice as many athletes once outperformed recording half as many twice in all 12 model-by-budget comparisons (+0.03 to +0.07; all p ≤ 0.003), and extra sets of the three pressing lifts were less effective than adding athletes (−0.02, p < 0.001). Recorded athletes were recognized better than new athletes; the difference narrowed from 0.27 to 0.09 as recording expanded but persisted (p < 0.001). Recording a new athlete on 2 exercises did not improve their other exercises (−0.008 to 0.000). Label-free calibration and self-training each added at most 0.04, about one additional recorded athlete, mainly when few athletes were recorded. Posture-only signals generalized to new athletes as well as the full signal (recorded-new difference 0.07 vs 0.09), whereas movement-only signals did not (0.14; p < 0.001).

**CONCLUSION:** In this dataset, spreading data collection across more athletes, one set each, was the most efficient use of a limited budget, with diminishing returns beyond about 12 athletes. Wrist posture during an exercise was shared across athletes, but movement patterns were individual and exercise-specific, so recording each new athlete on the exercises to be tracked remained beneficial and low-cost shortcuts were worth at most about one additional athlete.
