**Where Should a Strength Program Spend a Limited Wearable Data-Collection Budget?**

**PURPOSE:** Wrist-worn accelerometers can automatically log resistance training, but a program adopting one must first record labeled examples, and high-turnover programs repeatedly onboard new athletes. We examined how a limited recording budget should be allocated: across more athletes or more sets per athlete, and whether each new athlete must be recorded.

**METHODS:** Secondary analysis of a public dataset in which 35 adults performed 15 exercises (2,355 sets) wearing a single wrist-worn 3-axis accelerometer. Twenty-three participants recorded on at least 2 days were included. In each of 30 random draws, 6 participants were held out as never-recorded "new athletes", and simulated programs recorded 2 to 17 other participants for 1 or 2 sets per exercise on their first day. Recognition was tested on later days only, using a random forest, a convolutional neural network (CNN) and a long short-term memory network. We also tested recording a new athlete on only 2 exercises, and label-free calibration from one unlabeled session. The outcome was set-level macro-F1 (0-1). Participants were the unit of analysis (paired Wilcoxon tests; 95% bootstrap confidence intervals).

**RESULTS:** At an equal number of recorded sets, recording twice as many athletes once outperformed recording half as many twice in all 12 model-by-budget comparisons (+0.03 to +0.07; all p ≤ 0.003); for example, 8 athletes × 1 set vs 4 × 2: 0.78 vs 0.74 (random forest). For new athletes, random-forest macro-F1 rose from 0.64 (95% CI 0.60-0.67) with 2 recorded athletes to 0.84 (0.80-0.87) with 17; about 12 athletes captured 90% of this gain. Recorded athletes were recognized better than new athletes; the difference narrowed from 0.27 to 0.09 as recording expanded but persisted (p < 0.001). The random forest performed best with 2 recorded athletes and the CNN with 17 (+0.04 vs random forest, p = 0.001). Recording a new athlete on 2 exercises did not improve recognition of their other exercises (−0.008 to 0.000), whereas recording all 15 improved it by 0.06 to 0.13. Label-free calibration improved CNN macro-F1 by 0.04 with 2 recorded athletes (p < 0.001) but not with 17 (p = 0.07).

**CONCLUSION:** In this dataset, spreading data collection across more athletes, one set each, was the most efficient use of a limited budget, with diminishing returns beyond about 12 athletes. Recording each new athlete on the exercises to be tracked remained beneficial, and a brief 2-exercise onboarding did not substitute for it. These estimates can help programs plan data collection when adopting wearable exercise tracking.
