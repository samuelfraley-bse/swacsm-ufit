**Where Should a Strength Program Spend a Limited Wearable Data-Collection Budget?**

**PURPOSE:** Wrist-worn accelerometers can automatically log resistance training, but a program adopting one must first record labeled examples, and high-turnover programs repeatedly onboard new athletes. We examined how a limited recording budget should be allocated between athletes and sets, and whether low-cost shortcuts can substitute for recording athletes. We assumed a similar cost per recorded set, as when a whole roster lifts together; where each additional athlete requires a separate session, the advantage of recording more athletes may be smaller.

**METHODS:** Secondary analysis of a public dataset in which 35 adults performed 15 exercises (2,355 sets) wearing a single wrist-worn 3-axis accelerometer. Twenty-three participants recorded on at least 2 days were included. In each of 30 random draws, 6 participants were held out as never-recorded "new athletes", and simulated programs recorded 2 to 17 other participants for 1, 2 or 4 sets per exercise (athletes × sets). Recognition was tested on later days only, using a rule-based baseline, logistic regression (primary), a random forest and a convolutional neural network (CNN). Shortcuts tested were: recording a new athlete on only 2 exercises, extra sets of the most-confused lifts, label-free calibration from one unlabeled session, and self-training on unlabeled teammate data. The outcome was set-level macro-F1 (0-1; chance = 0.07). Each participant's scores were averaged across draws before testing, so participants were the unit of analysis (n = 23; paired Wilcoxon tests; 95% bootstrap confidence intervals).

**RESULTS:** For new athletes, logistic regression macro-F1 rose from 0.68 (95% CI 0.65-0.71) with 2 recorded athletes to 0.85 (0.82-0.88) with 17 (rule-based baseline 0.42-0.49). About 13 athletes captured 90% of this gain, with diminishing but continuing returns (0.81 at 8). At an equal number of recorded sets, more athletes with fewer sets each was better: 4 athletes × 1 set scored 0.75, 2 × 2 scored 0.70 and 1 × 4 scored 0.61 (4 × 1 vs 1 × 4: +0.14, all 23 participants higher, p < 0.001). The same ordering held at budgets of 8 and 16 sets and for the random forest and CNN (all p ≤ 0.004). Extra sets of the most-confused lifts were less effective than adding athletes (−0.02, p < 0.001). Recording a new athlete on 2 exercises did not improve their other exercises (−0.008 to 0.000). Label-free calibration and self-training each added at most 0.04, about one additional recorded athlete, and only when few athletes were recorded. For athletes who had been recorded, a second set added 0.02-0.03, whereas recording more teammates did not change their scores (0.91-0.92).

**CONCLUSION:** In this dataset, low-cost shortcuts did not substitute for recording athletes: recording 2 exercises did not carry over to other lifts, extra sets of confusable lifts were less effective than adding athletes, and calibration and self-training were each worth about one additional athlete. Spreading a limited budget across more athletes, one set each, gave the best recognition of new athletes, with diminishing returns beyond about 13. Extra sets help the current roster; extra athletes help future recruits.
